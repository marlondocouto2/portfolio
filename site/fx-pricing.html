Pricing experiment

The problem: a money-transfer company earns most of its money from a small fee built into the exchange rate (the markup). Raising it from 1.5% to 2.0% earns more on each transfer, but some customers will leave. Is it worth it?

What I built: an A/B test, where half the customers see the new price and half the old one, designed and analysed from start to finish. The customers are simulated, so their true reaction is known and every method can be checked against it.

The result: charging small senders 2.0% and large senders 1.5% gives +16% profit, against +11% for one price for everyone. Large senders are price-sensitive, so it's better to leave their price alone.

What I learned

  • An average can hide two opposite stories. The higher price wins overall, but large senders (12% of customers, a third of profit) shop around: they send 38% less and come back 39% less often.
  • So one price isn't the best answer. Keeping large senders at 1.5% roughly doubles the revenue gain and halves the volume lost.
  • How you run a test can change its answer. Checking results every day turns 5% false alarms into 27%. Testing two rules one after the other gives opposite answers depending on which one meets a busy period.
  • The next test should try a lower price for large senders. The estimated best is near 1.25%, below anything tested so far.

For the technical reader

  1. Design first: revenue per quoted user as the primary metric (non-senders count as $0), volume and repeat sending as guardrails, user-level randomization, a two-sided test.
  2. Simulated users with an S-shaped price response that is steeper for large senders, log-normal amounts and pre-test history, and both potential outcomes kept.
  3. Power analysis: about 88,000 users per arm for a 3% minimum detectable effect, checked with 300 simulated experiments.
  4. Readout: a sample-ratio check, Welch's difference in means, and CUPED with pre-test variables only.
  5. Sequential testing: O'Brien-Fleming boundaries at four weekly looks, on cohorts whose 14-day window has closed.
  6. Decision: profit vs revenue, a planned segment with an interaction test, a 15% non-inferiority guardrail on volume, and a logistic demand curve to find the best markup.
  7. Treasury designs: randomized switchbacks for a corridor's daily funding rule, and difference-in-differences with placebo tests.
