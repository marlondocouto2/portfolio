Should a remittance corridor raise its FX markup?

A money-transfer business earns most of its revenue on the FX markup. Raising it earns more per transfer but loses customers. This project designs and analyzes an A/B test of raising the U.S. → Mexico markup from 1.5% to 2.0%, from sample size to the launch decision, then tests a treasury funding rule where customers can't be randomized at all. Every user is simulated with both potential outcomes, so each method is checked against the true effect.

What the results show

  • The higher price wins on average, but the average hides two opposite stories. Small senders bring in more profit. Large senders, 12% of users but a third of profit, shop around: they send 38% less and their repeat sending falls by 39%.
  • Tiered pricing beats a single price. 2.0% for small senders and 1.5% for large ones roughly doubles the revenue gain and halves the volume loss, which passes a 15% non-inferiority guardrail that a single 2.0% fails.
  • Large senders' best markup is below today's price. The demand curve fit on three tested prices puts it near 1.25%. That's outside the tested range, so the next test needs a lower arm.
  • Design choices change the answer. Peeking daily turns a 5% false-positive rate into 27%. Running two funding rules one after the other gives opposite conclusions depending on which rule meets a demand surge.

How it works

  1. Design first: revenue per quoted user as the primary metric (non-senders count as $0), volume and repeat sending as guardrails, user-level randomization, a two-sided test.
  2. Simulated users with an S-shaped price response that is steeper for large senders, log-normal amounts and pre-test history, and both potential outcomes kept.
  3. Power analysis: about 88,000 users per arm for a 3% minimum detectable effect, checked with 300 simulated experiments.
  4. Readout: a sample-ratio check, Welch's difference in means, and CUPED with pre-test variables only.
  5. Sequential testing: O'Brien-Fleming boundaries at four weekly looks, on cohorts whose 14-day window has closed.
  6. Decision: profit vs revenue, a planned segment with an interaction test, non-inferiority guardrails, and a logistic demand curve to find the best markup.
  7. Treasury designs: randomized switchbacks for a corridor's daily funding rule, and difference-in-differences with placebo tests.

The design, with what to change in the next test, is in the experiment design. The funding rule tested here is built on the Project 1 forecast.
