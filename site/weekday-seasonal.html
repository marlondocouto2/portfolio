Project 1 · Forecasting

Daily payment forecast

The problem: a payments company has to set aside cash every morning for that day's payments. Too little and it runs short; too much and money sits idle.

What I built: a simple forecast. Take the recent average, then adjust it for the day of the week and for paydays.

The result: 35% more accurate than copying last week, so the company can keep $10.7M less cash on hand each day.

Why the reserve matters

This is where accuracy turns into money.

  • When payments come in higher than forecast, the company is short and has to find cash on the day, usually at a cost.
  • So it keeps a reserve on top of the forecast: enough to cover the shortfall on 95 days out of 100.
  • A better forecast misses by less, so it needs a smaller reserve for the same protection.

What I learned

  • A simple, well-built model beats the obvious shortcuts. Copying last week repeats last week's noise, and puts paydays on the wrong dates. An average ignores the weekly pattern completely.
  • It's almost as good as possible. Its error is close to the random noise built into the data, and it holds up a full month ahead.
  • What it misses: December's holiday peak, plus spikes and holidays. That's what Project 2 fixes.

For the technical reader

  1. Weekday factors, last 12 weeks: each day divided by the average of the week around it, so growth and slow seasonal bumps cancel out.
  2. Payday factors, last year: the same idea for five day-of-month buckets (1–3, 4–13, 14–16, 17 to month end, last 3 days).
  3. Estimated in turn for three passes, because a payday on a Friday would otherwise inflate Friday.
  4. Level: the average of the last 28 days after removing both effects. Forecast = level × weekday factor × payday factor.
  5. Evaluation: a walk-forward backtest over 2025, refitting at every origin, 1, 7 and 30 days ahead. The reserve is the 95th percentile of daily shortfalls.

Data: one simulated series, $50M a day growing 15% a year, with known weekday, payday and December effects and 6% noise. The full method with formulas is in the methodology.
